Look for criteria a script can't check, checks that can never fail, routes that send a problem to someone who can't fix it, and loops without a limit.

🪝 Who runs the gateThe stop hook calls run-stage-gate.sh when an agent finishes. The orchestrator calls the same script, so a gate can't be skipped by forgetting a step.
🔓 What unlocks the next stageA PASS entry in gate_log.jsonl for the current input hash. No entry, an old hash, or an error means the next stage doesn't start.
🚫 Who can't touch itAgents can't edit gates/ or the gate log. The after-edit hook flags any such edit and the stage fails its scope check.

📌 Recap

A pipeline that checks itself, within limits

  • Gates are only as good as their criteria: versioned, scriptable, able to fail, and routed to whoever can actually fix the problem.
  • The orchestrator, not a person, turns a FAIL into findings.json and a follow-up message. The producer patches its output instead of starting over.
  • Every loop stops on the first of: PASS, no progress, a broken invariant, the round limit, or the budget. Stopping early is a result, not a failure.
  • Cache keys decide reruns: only stages whose inputs changed, plus everything downstream. A criteria change re-runs the gate, not the agent.
  • Hooks enforce what rules only ask for, log every decision, and deny when they themselves fail.
  • One planned approval before the PR, by an authorised role, with a reason, tied to the artifact hash. Any later edit invalidates it.
