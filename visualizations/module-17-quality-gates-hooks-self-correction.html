§3 · Hooks for validation, policy, logging, lifecycle

Stand in the Test Generator's shoes and try things

A hook is a script you own. Cursor runs it automatically at a set point in the agent's work, such as before a shell command or after a file edit. It can allow, deny, or ask, and it writes a log line each time. The model can't ignore it or forget it. Click an action to see which hook fires and what it decides.

§4 · Human-in-the-loop checkpoints

Protection vs. approval fatigue

People are the slowest and most expensive check, and the one with the final say. If you add too many checkpoints, people start approving without reading, and the checkpoints stop protecting anything. Switch checkpoints on and watch both meters.

§5 · Bounded retries

A retry counter alone isn't enough

Correction loops go wrong in predictable ways. Pick one, choose which guards are on, and step through the rounds. The progress, invariant, and budget checks often stop a loop earlier than the retry limit would.

📌 Recap

From "you are the gate" to controls that run on their own

  • A gate checks a stage's output between stages. Its criteria are objective, observable, actionable, cheapest first, and versioned. It returns PASS, FAIL, or NEEDS_HUMAN.
  • An LLM judge never overrules a failure from a scripted check. Stop at the first FAIL.
  • Send each FAIL to the stage that caused it, with structured findings. Have it patch its output rather than regenerate it, and re-run only the stages downstream of it.
  • A hook checks an action inside the agent's work, and always runs. Use rules to explain, and hooks to enforce and log.
  • Put human checkpoints where the impact is high or the action can't be undone, and give approvers a decision packet, not a chat transcript. Too many checkpoints lead to approval fatigue.
  • Bound every loop with max rounds, a progress check, invariants, and a budget. Log every decision, and if a check fails to run, stop (NEEDS_HUMAN or deny), never PASS.
